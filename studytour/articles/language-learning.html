Learning Through Language.

An intensive introduction to Japanese language, communication and intercultural understanding.

BY MATT ARMSTRONG NOVEMBER 2026 LANGUAGE & COMMUNICATION

IN THIS ENTRY

An Intensive Introduction. Learning To Communicate. More Than Translating Words. Listening Before Responding.

Having completed five days of intensive Japanese language classes and several language exchanges with local students, I have come away with a greater appreciation for the role language plays in connecting people. While I already had some experience studying Japanese before arriving in Japan, this week gave me the opportunity to put that knowledge into practice in a much more interactive environment. Reflecting on the experience, I found that understanding the language and being able to use it naturally are two quite different challenges.

01 / THE LEARNING EXPERIENCE

An intensive introduction.

Over five days, we participated in intensive Japanese language classes, with each session running for approximately two hours. Although this was a relatively short period of instruction, I found the experience particularly interesting because I was not approaching the language as a complete beginner. Throughout the year, I had already spent several months studying Japanese with a teacher through Preply, alongside using the Pimsleur audio course to practise listening and speaking.

As a result, many of the concepts introduced during the classes were things I had encountered before. I generally understood the foundations being taught, which made it interesting to revisit familiar material in a more concentrated learning environment. The pace felt good, although I do wonder whether this was partly because of the understanding I had developed through my previous studies.

In some ways, it felt as though several months of learning had been condensed into an intensive ten-hour crash course. Having that existing foundation helped me follow the lessons, but it also highlighted how much more there is to learn when it comes to actually using the language.

02 / CHALLENGES & PROGRESS

Learning to communicate.

One of the biggest challenges I noticed was the difference between understanding a Japanese concept and being able to apply it on the spot. During a lesson, it is possible to take your time, think through a sentence and work out how the different parts fit together. In a conversation, however, there is much less time to think. You need to recall vocabulary, construct sentences and understand the other person's response while the interaction is still happening.

I still find it challenging to communicate everything I want to say. There are so many Japanese words that I have yet to learn, and sometimes I understand the general direction of a conversation without having the vocabulary to properly express my own thoughts. Despite this, I definitely felt more confident by the end of the week. Having regular opportunities to practise helped make speaking feel a little less intimidating.

One small achievement that stood out to me was actually calling a restaurant and ordering pizza over the phone in Japanese. It might seem like a fairly ordinary task, but it was something I would have been much less confident attempting before the classes. Being able to put what I had learned into practice in a real situation was a satisfying reminder that even a relatively short period of focused learning can make a difference.

03 / LANGUAGE IN EVERYDAY LIFE

More than translating words.

Outside the classroom, we also had several opportunities to take part in language exchanges with our TCU buddies. Local students would join us after classes, and we would spend time together over lunch, trying to use what we had learned to communicate with one another.

I found these interactions to be one of the most enjoyable parts of the language learning experience. While the classes provided the foundations, the exchanges gave us an opportunity to use Japanese in a more natural setting. Sometimes professors would join in as well, and their enthusiasm for encouraging us to speak Japanese with the local students was noticeable. It was clear that they genuinely wanted us to make the most of these opportunities and engage with one another beyond the classroom.

What made the conversations particularly enjoyable was how easily we could find common ground. We spent time talking about anime, manga and music, which gave us some familiar topics to connect over. We also discussed our university studies and what we hoped to do in the future. I enjoyed learning about the interests and aspirations of the students, while also having the opportunity to share a little about my own.

These conversations reminded me that communication is not necessarily dependent on having perfect language skills. Even when we struggled to find the right words, having shared interests and a genuine willingness to communicate helped keep the conversations going. It was a good reminder that language is not just something to study, but a tool that allows people to build relationships and understand one another.

04 / INTERCULTURAL LEADERSHIP

Listening before responding.

Looking back on the experience, I think one of the biggest lessons has been recognising the importance of patience when communicating across language barriers. When you cannot immediately express what you are thinking, or when you do not fully understand what someone else is saying, it becomes important to slow down, listen carefully and find another way to communicate.

This connects with the idea of intercultural communication and the importance of being adaptable when interacting with people from different backgrounds. It is easy to become focused on getting your own message across, but meaningful communication also depends on making an effort to understand the other person. Sometimes that means accepting that you will make mistakes, asking someone to repeat themselves or finding a simpler way to explain what you mean.

I think these are valuable skills beyond learning Japanese. In software engineering, particularly when working within international teams, people can have very different experiences, communication styles and levels of familiarity with a particular subject. Being able to listen, adapt and communicate patiently can make a significant difference to how effectively people work together.

I have definitely come away from this experience feeling more confident in my ability to use Japanese, although I am also aware of how much I still have to learn. I am looking forward to continuing to expand my vocabulary, improving my ability to express more complex thoughts and having more conversations with Japanese speakers. If anything, these five days have reinforced my motivation to keep studying, particularly now that I have experienced how rewarding it can be to use the language to connect with other people.
